Calculate oil flow before choosing shims

A reading sample from Chapter 1, Section 1.2 and Worked example 1A. Equations are reformatted for this web page; their book numbers are retained.

Picture a sealed piston in a straight cylinder. A rod of the same diameter leaves both ends of the piston. We call this a double-rod test fixture. Both pressure sides therefore have the same active area. Oil pushed out of one side passes through a valve and enters the other.

There is no gas reservoir in this example and no net oil displacement caused by the rod entering the cylinder. The rod volume entering one end equals the rod volume leaving the other end. The piston still moves oil between its two chambers. It is a useful teaching fixture; it is not a generic model of every shock or fork.

First, define the quantities

  • Dp = cylinder bore diameter, in metres (m).
  • Dr = through-rod diameter, in metres (m).
  • A = active piston area, in square metres (m²).
  • v = shaft velocity, in metres per second (m/s).
  • Q = oil volume flow rate, in cubic metres per second (m³/s).
  • π = pi, approximately 3.14159.

Convert millimetres to metres by dividing by 1,000.

(1.2)   A = π × (Dp² − Dr²) ÷ 4

Square each diameter, subtract the rod result from the bore result, then multiply by pi and divide by four.

(1.3)   Q = A × v

Multiply active area by shaft velocity to find the oil flow.

Worked example 1A

For a 36 mm bore, a 12 mm through-rod and a shaft velocity of 0.300 m/s:

  1. Convert the diameters: 0.036 m and 0.012 m.
  2. Calculate area: π × (0.036² − 0.012²) ÷ 4 = 0.000904779 m².
  3. Calculate flow: 0.000904779 × 0.300 = 0.000271434 m³/s.
  4. Multiply by 60,000 to convert to litres per minute: 16.286 L/min.

What this tells us: the valve must pass that volume at this shaft speed. We have not chosen pressure or shim thickness yet. Those require the valve's resistance to flow.

The complete chapter continues through pressure, shim opening and a complete operating point. The teaching examples require measurement and physical testing before a real design can be validated.

From oil flow to shim-stack design

Written by Craig Dixon of Mountain Race Shop™, this specialist textbook takes you from the basics of oil flow and pressure through to designing shim stacks and checking their performance on a suspension dyno.

The book combines advanced engineering with plain-language explanations, defined symbols, numbered equations and worked examples. It starts with the foundations, then builds towards the calculations needed to make informed decisions about fork and shock damping.

What you will learn

  • Calculate oil flow, pressure differences and damping forces.
  • Understand the damping produced by bleed holes, piston ports and valve blocks.
  • Calculate shim bending and assess changes in shim diameter, thickness, number and arrangement.
  • Balance damping between fork base valves and mid-valves.
  • Understand traditional spring and trampoline mid-valves, including how to choose float.
  • Assess gas pressure and internal compensation spring (ICS) requirements to help control cavitation.
  • Account for oil viscosity, temperature, wheel rate and suspension linkage ratio.
  • Turn the motorcycle, riding conditions and rider’s needs into measurable damping targets.
  • Compare calculated results with dyno measurements and use the findings to refine a design.

Built for learning and workshop reference

Includes numbered equations, step-by-step worked examples, diagrams, a guide to reading the mathematics, a glossary, equation and subject indexes, and Harvard-style references.

Suitable for suspension technicians, race mechanics, engineering students and riders who want a deeper understanding of damping design. No previous shim-stack design experience is assumed; the later chapters build on the earlier explanations.

Calculations provide a starting point for design. Measurement, careful assembly and dyno testing remain part of confirming how a particular valve performs.
