Read before you buy

A reading sample from the introduction

An extract from The Suspension Engineer’s Handbook, by Craig Dixon.

Suspension gets talked about in all sorts of ways.

Riders say a bike feels planted, nervous, harsh, lazy, balanced, dead, lively, soft, spiky, or vague. Tuners say it rides high, blows through, packs down, kicks, chatters, wallows, or holds up. Most of those descriptions are useful, but on their own they do not explain much. They describe the result, not the cause.

That is the problem this book is trying to solve.

For years, suspension has often been treated as though it lives somewhere between black art, folklore, and trial-and-error. In one sense that is understandable, because no two riders describe feel in exactly the same way, and no two bikes respond to changes in exactly the same way. But underneath all of that, the hardware is still doing real and measurable things. Oil is being displaced. Pressure is being generated. Shims are bending. Springs are storing energy. Gas volumes are changing. Loads are moving through the tyre, wheel, chassis, and rider.

So while suspension can feel mysterious, it is not actually mysterious.

It is a mechanical and hydraulic system. If we understand the forces involved, the flow paths involved, and the timing involved, then many of the things riders describe as feel can be traced back to something real in the hardware.
